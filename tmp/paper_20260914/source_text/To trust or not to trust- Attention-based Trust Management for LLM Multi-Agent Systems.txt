Abstract
Large Language Model-based Multi-Agent Sys-
tems (LLM-MAS) have demonstrated strong
capabilities in solving complex tasks but re-
main vulnerable when agents receive unreli-
able messages. This vulnerability stems from a
fundamental gap: LLM agents treat all incom-
ing messages equally without evaluating their
trustworthiness. While some existing studies
approach trustworthiness, they focus on a sin-
gle type of harmfulness rather than analyze it
in a holistic approach from multiple trustwor-
thiness perspectives. We address this gap by
proposing a comprehensive definition of trust-
worthiness inspired by human communication
theory (Grice, 1975). Our definition identi-
fies six orthogonal trust dimensions that pro-
vide interpretable measures of trustworthiness.
Building on this definition, we introduce the
Attention Trust Score (A-Trust), a lightweight,
attention-based method for evaluating the trust-
worthiness of messages. We then develop a
principled trust management system (TMS) for
LLM-MAS that supports both message -level
and agent-level trust assessments. Experiments
across diverse multi -agent settings and tasks
demonstrate that our TMS significantly im-
proves robustness against malicious inputs.
1 Introduction
Large Language Model-based Multi-Agents (LLM-
MAS) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities
in complex problem solving and task planning (Li
et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024). They advance nu-
merous applications such as code generation (Liu
et al., 2024; Hong et al.; Qian et al., 2023), mathe-
matical reasoning (Lei et al., 2024; He et al., 2024),
and scientific simulations (Zheng et al., 2023; Tang
et al., 2023). Based on (Tran et al., 2025), the
effectiveness of LLM-MAS is largely driven by the
collaboration and communication among agents

with diverse roles and expertise, and informative
messages can improve performance. However, un-
reliable or misleading messages can corrupt rea-
soning and cause harmful or incorrect behaviors
of the system. Recent studies have shown that
LLM-MAS are highly sensitive to incorrect infor-
mation (Lee and Tiwari, 2024), and vulnerable to
malicious attacks, including the injection of de-
ceptive messages by compromised agents (Zhang
et al., 2024a; Ju et al., 2024) and communication
interception attacks (He et al., 2025).
A fundamental reason for these vulnerabilities
is that LLM agents do not apply the skepticism
humans typically use to vet information (Li et al.,
2023; Wu et al., 2023). LLMs treat every input
as part of the context without checking whether
it is reliable or appropriate—a notion researchers
broadly refer to as the “trustworthiness” of mes-
sages (a broad concept). As a result, low-quality or
malicious messages are processed indiscriminately,
degrading performance and sometimes triggering
unsafe behaviors. This problem is especially press-
ing as Agent2Agent protocol (A2A) (Google, 2025)
becomes increasingly popular. Future LLM-MAS
may aggregate agents from diverse and potentially
unverified sources. This highlights the urgent need
for a principledtrustworthiness evaluationof in-
coming messages, and a correspondingtrust man-
agement systemthat governs how agents respond
to messages, i.e., whether to accept, reject, or ques-
tion, based on the underlying context.
However, building trustworthiness evaluation
methods and trust management systems poses chal-
lenges. First, the concept of trustworthiness is in-
herently broad. While prior works have examined
specific aspects—such as factual correctness (Ra-
jan et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024) and logical
consistency (Lim and Perrault, 2024; Pan et al.,
2024)—these efforts typically address each aspect
in isolation. In LLM-MAS settings, however, unre-
liable/malicious messages can be diverse, involv-

ing different aspects of trustworthiness violations.
Second, most existing approaches rely on prompt-
ing LLMs (Pan et al., 2024) or external verifier
tools (Rajan et al., 2024). However, the former suf-
fers from hallucination and inconsistency (Huang
et al., 2025), and the latter introduces latency and
depends heavily on the quality of external datasets
or systems (Gou et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023).
To fill this gap, we introduce the first compre-
hensive definition of “trustworthiness” tailored to
LLM-MAS. Building on human communication
research (Grice, 1975), we distill trust into six di-
mensions—factual accuracy, logical consistency,
relevance, bias, language quality, and clarity. Each
dimension captures a specific way in which a mes-
sage or agent can inspire trust, and together they
provide a structured vocabulary for reasoning about
why and when to accept information from other
agents. This multi-dimensional definition enables
clear and interpretable measurement of trustworthi-
ness and lays the foundation for systematic trust
management, leading to more robust systems.
Building on our trustworthiness definition and
drawing on recent research into multi-head atten-
tion in LLMs (Olsson et al., 2022; Hung et al.,
2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Gould et al., 2023), we
show that internal attention patterns can be used
to gauge the trustworthiness of inter -agent mes-
sages. To explore this, we create the Trust Violation
dataset, where each message is crafted to violate
exactly one of the six trust dimensions. Analysis
of this dataset reveals that different violations pro-
duce distinctive attention patterns and that certain
attention heads specialize in detecting specific vi-
olations. Leveraging these insights, we propose
A-Trust, an attention -based method that quanti-
fies each trust dimension without relying on ex-
ternal verifiers. We then introduce atrust man-
agement system (TMS)that operates at the agent
level to monitor inter-agent communication, sup-
port trust-aware action policies, and maintain dy-
namic trust records for each agent. Experiments
demonstrate the effectiveness of both A-Trust and
TMS.
2 Multi-head Attention Analysis towards
Trustworthiness Evaluation
In this section, we first introduce the six dimensions
to measure trustworthiness and the corresponding
new dataset, then provide comprehensive analy-
sis on the role of multi-head attention towards the
trustworthiness evaluation of input messages.
2.1 Trust Dimensions
A key challenge in determining whether an input
message is trustworthy or not lies in defining clear
and comprehensive evaluation criteria. While prior
work has explored specific aspects of trustwor-
thiness—such as factual accuracy (Zheng et al.,
2024) and logical consistency (Lim and Perrault,
2024)—these dimensions alone are insufficient for
a holistic assessment, especially when inter-agent
messages are highly diverse. To address this limi-
tation, we draw inspiration from studies of human
communication, particularly the cooperative princi-
ple (Grice, 1975) in social science and linguistics,
and proposesix trust dimensionsthat collectively
capture the trustworthiness of a message within its
context. In particular, we consider:
Trust Dimensions
• Factual accuracy: whether the message is verifi-
ably true and supported by reliable evidence.
• Logical consistency: whether the content is inter-
nally coherent and free from contradictions, without
logical fallacies or conflicting statements.
• Relevance(to context): whether the information
addresses the prompt, question, or surrounding dis-
course, without off-topic or tangential content.
• Bias: whether the message maintains objectivity,
avoids favoritism or prejudice, and presents infor-
mation without improper emotional, ideological, or
cultural bias.
• Clarity: whether the expression is unambiguous,
concise, and easy to understand, without vagueness,
redundancy or unnecessary complexity.
• Quality: whether the writing is fluent, grammati-
cally correct, and stylistically appropriate.
The six dimensions provide a structured, com-
prehensive framework for evaluating the trustwor-
thiness of input messages (Li et al., 2011), enabling
fine-grained analysis. To menasure these dimen-
sions, we construct aTrust Violationdataset with
>20k samples across six violation classes plus a
benign class. Unlike prior datasets where untrust-
worthy examples often conflate multiple violations
and lack explicit labels (e.g., (Huang et al., 2024;
Yu et al., 2024)), each untrustworthy sample in ours
is crafted to breach exactly one dimension while
satisfying the others; the benign class contains no
violations and serves as a reference. This design
supports fine-grained analysis of LLM behavior
and reveals dimension-specific attention patterns
critical for trustworthiness evaluation.
To build the dataset, we reuse prior resources
for factual accuracy (FEVER (Thorne et al., 2018))
PAGE 3
and bias (StereoSet (Nadeem et al., 2020)), retain-
ing only samples that do not violate other dimen-
sions. For the remaining dimensions—logical con-
sistency, relevance, clarity and quality, which lack
coverage in existing corpora, we generate new data
with GPT-4o, using prompts that enforce each tar-
get dimension. Each sample is contextualized as
a realistic LLM-MAS communication: the mes-
sage M (incoming to an agent) is paired with a
context C containing a benign agent profile and
task-specific instructions. Tasks are drawn from
math reasoning, code generation, and general QA.

3 TMS: Trust Management System for
LLM-MAS
To ensure secure and reliable collaboration in LLM-
MAS, we introduce a dedicated Trust Management
System (TMS) to monitor inter-agent communica-
tions and evaluate the trustworthiness of messages.
The primary goals of our TMS are to (1) evalu-
ate trustworthiness of messages, (2) support trust-
aware actions, and (3) maintain agent-level trust
records for flexible utilization. An illustration can
be found in Figure 4. In the following, we present
details of TMS and show how it is combined with
our proposed trust measurements.
Message-level trust evaluation. The trust manage-
ment process begins at the message level. For every
message communicating among agents, the system
assesses its trustworthiness using the integrated
trust measurement. In particular, the system ex-
tracts the message’s attention matrix from an LLM
given context (containing benign agent profile, task
description and previous messages) and applies pre-
trained A-trust models (ff act, ..., fclarity ) (same as
Section 2) to evaluate six trust dimensions and ob-
tain raw trust scores(s f act, ..., sclarity ). This anal-
ysis provides interpretable feedback towards the
trustworthiness of the messages, allowing further
filtering of untrustworthy messages.
Trust-aware Action Policy. Given the assessment
of a message, the system then determines how to
act upon the messages, such as transmitting to the
target agent, directly filtering it or requesting fur-
ther verification. In this work, we propose a general
rule-based decision criterion leveraging A-Trust
scores. Specifically, we apply individual decision
thresholds to each A-trust dimension, denoted as
τ= (τ f act, ...τclarity ). For each incoming mes-
sage, if the score for one trust dimension exceeds
its corresponding threshold (i.e., score > τ ), the
message is flagged as untrustworthy and is filtered
out; otherwise, it is accepted. While the specific
goal in real practice depends on particular demands,
we consider the following evaluation metric as an
example to demonstrate how the proposed system
works. We define key evaluation metrics to guide
threshold selection. For each trust dimensionr,
True Violation Rate (TVR): P(score r > τ r|violation), the
probability of correctly flagging a violated message.
False Violation Rate (FVR): P(score r > τ r|non-violation),
the probability of falsely rejecting a benign message.
True Benign Rate (TBR): P(score r ≤τ r|non-violation),
the probability of correctly accepting a benign message.
False Benign Rate (FBR): P(score r ≤τ r|violation), the
probability of falsely accepting a violated message.
Given the above definitions, we aim for high
TVR and low FBR, ensuring that malicious mes-
sages are detected with minimal rejections of be-
nign ones. We also seek for low FVR and high
TBR, ensuring that benign messages are not unnec-
essarily blocked. To keep a balance between these
two objectives, we select thresholds that maximize
the following objective: maxτr T V R+T BR−

4.1 Setups
Multi-agent System. Following prior
work (Huang et al., 2024; He et al., 2025),
we adopt the Camel framework (Li et al., 2023)
as our primary testbed. To ensure comprehensive
evaluation, we experiment with several representa-
tive communicationstructures: (1) Chain, where
agents are connected sequentially; (2) Tree, a
bottom-up hierarchy where sibling agents under
the same parent can exchange messages with
each other before sending a summarized message
to their parent; (3) Complete, where all agents
are connected with each other. Unless otherwise
specified, we instantiate each setting with 4 agents
in Chain, 2 parents with 2 children each in Tree,
and 4 agents in Complete, and use LLaMA3.1-8B-
Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the backbone
LLM. We also include MetaGPT (Hong et al.),
a real-world software-development multi-agent
framework, as a case study, and use GPT-4o as the
backbone LLM for its code generation capability.
Attacks. We test on multiple attacks designed
for LLM-MAS. (1) AiTM (He et al., 2025), a com-
munication attack to intercept and modify inter-
agent messages in real time. We adopt the Denial-
of-Service (DoS) version in our experiments. (2)
AutoTransformand AutoInject (Huang et al., 2024).
AutoTransform attacks the agent profile to intro-
duce stealthy errors; while AutoInject injects errors
in the outputs of agents. (3) NetSafe (Yu et al.,
2024), an attack that injects faulty or misleading
messages. Details are in Appendix C.1.

Datasets. Our experiments are conducted on
various datasets representing diverse tasks. (1)
MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a) is a multitask lan-
guage understanding dataset. We use the physics
subset (excluded in Trust Violation) for evalua-
tion. (2) StratagyQA (Geva et al., 2021) is a QA
benchmark with implicit reasoning strategies. (3)
MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021b; Lightman
et al., 2023) contains a subset (size 500) of math
reasoning problems in MATH dataset (Hendrycks
et al., 2021b). (4) MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) con-
tains 974 programming tasks to evaluate the code-
generation ability.
Trust management details. We apply A-Trust
for message-level trust evaluation. By default, we
use Llama3.1-8B-Instruct to extract attention matri-
ces for each message given a benign agent profile
(shared across agents), the task description, and
the message history. The resulting attention vec-
tors are passed to trust models trained on the Trust
Violation dataset to compute scores across six di-
mensions. For the trust-aware action policy, we
use predefined thresholds (derived from the Trust
Violation dataset) to decide whether a message vio-
lates each trust dimension. For the factual dimen-
sion, we adopt a double-threshold strategy with
an external verifier (see Section 3). If a message
falls into the uncertain range, we invoke Gemini-
2.0-flash—linked to Google Search—as the factual
verifier. For other dimensions, we use a single-
threshold strategy. Finally, we maintain agent-level
trust records, storing both raw trust scores and bi-
nary violation flags per message.6
Baselines. Besides A-Trust, we integrate two
alternative trust evaluation methods into TMS: the
perplexity-based score and the prompt-based score,
both introduced in Section 2.3. The perplexity-
based score is used solely to distinguish between

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate the perfor-
mance of the proposed TMS with multiple metrics.
(1)Message detection rate(MDR): The proportion
of malicious messages correctly identified by the
system. This metric assesses the system’s ability
to evaluate the trustworthiness of messages. (2)

licious agents successfully identified by the system.
This metric evaluates how effectively the agent-
level trust records capture and flag persistent ma-
licious behavior. (3)End-to-end attack success
rate(ASR): The attack success rate of each attack

TMS. This metric measures the effectiveness of

4.3 Agent-level trust record utilization
Besides message-level filtering, we also evaluate
the performance of agent-level trust records in the
system. As an illustration, we implement a fully
automatic intervention policy based on these trust
records. For each agent, we examine the violation
rate—the proportion of messages flagged as vi-
olations—for the most recent 20 queries. If the
violation rate exceeds 80%, the agent is identi-
fied as malicious and removed from the system.
We present the results in Table 3. It is evident
that this trust record–based policy ishighly effec-
tive in identifying malicious agents, achieving
a 100% Agent Detection Rate (ADR) across all
evaluated attacks. When combined with A-Trust,
it produces substantial reductions in Attack Suc-
cess Rate (ASR). For example, under the AiTM
attack on StrategyQA (Chain structure), ASR drops
from 90.3% (No trust) to 1.8% (A-Trust), and in
the Tree structure, from 80.5% to 1.6%. These
findings indicate that persistent malicious behavior
can be reliably captured and mitigated by agent-
level trust records in the proposed system. At the
same time, the policypreserves system utility.
Under clean conditions, no benign agents are in-
correctly flagged (0% ADR). Overall, the results
demonstrate the potential of trust records to enable
effective automated interventions for trustworthy
LLM-MAS deployments.
5 Related works
As LLM-MAS are increasingly adopted in high-
stakes applications, their security vulnerabilities
have drawn growing attention. Prior work reveals
risks arising from psychological manipulation, task
persuasion, malicious agents, system structure,
and communication channels (Zhang et al., 2024b;
PAGE 9
Amayuelas et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024; Huang
et al., 2024; He et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024a;
Lee and Tiwari, 2024). On the defense side, ef-
forts include memory protection, agent auditing,
and security benchmarks (Wei et al., 2025; Luo
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). However, exist-
ing studies lack a principled understanding of why
inter-agent communication is vulnerable and how
to systematically build robust LLM-MAS, motivat-
ing our focus on trust-aware communication and
management mechanisms.
Due to page limit, we postpone the details of
other related works to Section A in the Appendix.
6 Conclusion
In this work, we decompose the concept of trust-
worthiness into six measurable dimensions to ana-
lyze the trustworthiness of inter-agent communica-
tion in LLM-MAS, create a corresponding dataset,
and further develop A-Trust, an attention-based
method to evaluate the message trustworthiness.
Furthermore, we introduce TMS, which leverages
A-Trust to monitor and enhance the trustworthiness
of LLM-MAS. Experiments verify the effective-
ness of the proposed methods.
Limitations and future directions
We identify two limitations. First, as noted in Re-
mark 1, while we focus on six core dimensions of
trustworthiness, other factors may influence inter-
agent communication in LLM-MAS. Scaling up
TMS to incorporate these additional factors could
provide a more comprehensive analysis. Second,
our current experiments assume relatively balanced
resource allocation across agents. Future stud-
ies could investigate how resource imbalances af-
fect trustworthiness assessment and system perfor-
mance.

A Related works
LLM-based Multi-agent System.LLM-MAS en-
hance the reasoning and task-solving capabilities
of large language models by enabling collaboration
among multiple specialized agents. This design
mirrors human group dynamics, where agents pro-
cess each other’s outputs to perform subsequent
reasoning and actions (Li et al., 2023; Wu et al.,
2023; He et al., 2024). Despite Camel (Li et al.,
2023) and MetaGPT (Hong et al.) used in this
work, many multi-agent frameworks and applica-
tions are developed. AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023)
offers a generic framework enabling conversations
among agents; AgentScope (Gao et al., 2024) is a
flexible and robust developer-centric multi-agent
platform; ChatDev (Qian et al., 2023) is a software
developing framework that reduces hallucinations
via conversations among agents in different phases.
LLM-MAS have shown great potential in a wide
range of domains, including software development
(Hong et al.), social science (Gao et al., 2023), em-
bodied ai (Guo et al., 2024), role-play gaming (Xu
et al., 2023).
LLM-MAS security. Despite the success of
LLM-MAS, security problems also attract attention
as it has been increasingly popular in high-stake
application. PsySafe (Zhang et al., 2024b) provides
an attacking and evaluation framework that analyze
the vulnerabilities from psychological manipula-
tion involving negative personalities. (Amayuelas
et al., 2024) demonstrates how agents can be per-
suaded to abandon tasks during collaboration. (Yu
et al., 2024) and (Huang et al., 2024) investigates
how malicious agents do harm to the system and
how agent structures can affect such attacks. (He
et al., 2025) reveals the vulnerability within LLM-
MAS’s communication mechanism. More attacks
can be found in (Zhang et al., 2024a; Lee and Ti-
wari, 2024). On the defense side, some methods
are proposed to protect agent memory (Wei et al.,
2025), evaluations on the security of LLM agents
(Luo et al., 2025) as well as some benchmarks
(Chen et al., 2025). However, there lacks a deeper
understanding of why LLM-MAS are vulnerable
to such attacks and approaches to build more ro-
bust system. Therefore, in this work, we study the
trustworthiness of multi-agent communication and
propose a trust management framework for trust-
worthy LLM-MAS development.
Trust in non-LLM multi-agent systems. Trust
and trustworthiness have been extensively studied
PAGE 13
across multiple communities, with a rich body of
work providing formal and mathematical founda-
tions. Early and influential frameworks such as
(Jsang, 2018) formalize trust as a combination of
belief, disbelief, and uncertainty, offering princi-
pled tools for reasoning under uncertainty and ag-
gregating opinions in probabilistic settings. These
formulations have been widely adopted in domains
where trust is explicitly represented and updated
through well-defined belief states. More recent
work has explored trust quantification in deep learn-
ing systems. For example, prior studies (Cheng
et al., 2020) model trustworthiness and opinion
in neural networks by explicitly estimating con-
fidence, uncertainty, or reliability of model pre-
dictions, often grounded in probabilistic reason-
ing or Bayesian interpretations. Related efforts
in multi-agent systems (Cheng et al., 2021) pro-
pose general trust frameworks that treat trust as an
agent-level construct, maintained through explicit
trust scores, reputation updates, or long-term in-
teraction histories. Similarly, trust-aware neural
architecture design (Cheng et al., 2022) has been
investigated by incorporating trustworthiness mea-
sures into model evaluation or training objectives,
primarily at the model or component level. Our
work is complementary to these lines of research.
Rather than proposing a new mathematical founda-
tion of trust or redefining trust as an explicit belief
or reputation variable, we focus on an operational,
message-level characterization of trustworthiness
tailored to attention-based LLM multi-agent sys-
tems. In such systems, trust is often exercised im-
plicitly through message aggregation mechanisms
(e.g., attention), without explicit trust states, belief
updates, or long-term reputation tracking. Our goal
is therefore to bridge classical trust notions with
modern LLM-based agent architectures by identi-
fying trust-relevant message properties that can be
directly leveraged within attention-based aggrega-
tion, while remaining agnostic to the underlying
mathematical formalization of trust itself.

B.4 Potential adaptive attacks
We provide a discussion 7 on potential attacks tar-
geting the proposed TMS, focusing on two kinds
of attacks where the attacker is aware of the trust
mechanism: (1) trust evasion attacks, where the
adversary attempts to craft stealthy malicious mes-
sages that bypass trust evaluation, and (2) trust
camouflage attacks, where the adversary initially
behaves benignly to build trust before launching
malicious actions.
For trust evasion attacks, the attacker would need
direct access to TMS to iteratively optimize mes-
sages based on feedback from trust scores. How-
ever, this assumption is overly strong and unreal-
istic in practice, as the trust management is em-
bedded within the multi-agent system and is not
externally accessible. For trust camouflage attacks,
the attacker seeks to delay detection by initially
sending benign messages. Nonetheless, our system
mitigates this risk through its two-layer defense:
the message-level trust evaluation effectively filters
out malicious content, while the agent-level trust
records track behavior over time. As demonstrated
in Section 4.3, even short-term bursts of malicious
activity can be identified based on accumulated
violations, ensuring that camouflaged agents are
exposed once they deviate. Based on these discus-
sions, our system is both effective against existing
attacks and robust for adaptive attacks.

C Details of settings in Section 4
C.1 Attack description
We present more details on attacking methods used
in experiments.
AiTM(He et al., 2025). Agent-in-the-Middle
(AiTM) is a communication attack that intercepts
the communicating messages between the victim
agent and other agents, and modify them to achieve
malicious goals. In this paper, we implement the
Denial-of-Service (DoS) version, where the mali-
cious goal is to induce the system to refuse solving
the task. The attack success rate is measure by the
proportion of queries that is refused (e.g. “I can
not assist” and etc) by the system. We implement
this attack by adopting the prompt provided in the
original paper.
AutoTrans& AutoInj. These are two attacks
proposed in (Huang et al., 2024). AutoTrans lever-
ages LLMs to transform a benign agent profile
into a malicious profile; while AutoInj leverages
LLMs to rewrite messages from the victim agent
to directly inject malicious information. The attack
success rate is measured by the error rate of solv-
ing the task, i.e. 1-accuracy. We implement these
attacks following the original code.
NetSafe(Yu et al., 2024). NetSafe is a suite con-
taining attacks and evaluation on LLM-MAS’s ro-
bustness, especially on the topological structure of
the system. We adopt the Misinformation Injection
version and directly use the provided prompt to
implement the attack. The attack success rate is
also measured by the error rate.

E Additional discussion
E.1 Maliciousness covered by Trust Violation
and A-Trust
(1) Coverage of maliciousness in our experiments.
In our experiments on both agent-based bench-
marks and real-world applications, we evaluate
A-trust under a range of natural and adversarial
settings. These include attacks that are not limited
to a single trust violation and, in many cases, are
not explicitly seen in the constructed trust-violation
dataset. Across these settings, we observe that A-
trust can effectively reduce the influence of mali-
cious messages, suggesting that a substantial por-
tion of practical malicious behavior can be handled
by the proposed approach.
(2) Maliciousness that may be missed. We do
not attempt to enumerate or exhaustively catego-
rize all possible types of maliciousness. We agree,
however, that stealthy or highly subtle malicious
behaviors, which only weakly violate trust dimen-
sions or deliberately mimic benign messages, may
be more difficult to detect. In such cases, lowering
the trust threshold can help surface weaker signals
of maliciousness, but this may also reduce sys-
tem utility by suppressing benign messages. This
trade-off highlights an important balance between
sensitivity and robustness, which we view as an
open direction for future work.
(3) When A-trust is expected to succeed vs.
when caution is needed. A-trust is most reliable
when malicious messages exhibit clear and observ-
able violations, such as obvious factual inaccura-
cies, logical incoherence, or irrelevant content. In
contrast, when malicious content is subtle, well-
formed, and closely resembles benign messages,
A-trust may partially mix such signals with benign
ones. In these scenarios, while detection becomes
more challenging, the impact of such weak mali-
cious signals on the overall system behavior is also
typically limited. Nevertheless, these cases war-
rant caution and motivate complementary defenses
beyond message-level trust scoring.
